How the system decides who has the ball

For every frame of the match, 25 times a second, the system must say one of three things: Barcelona have it, Athletic have it, or the ball is dead. It never watches the pictures. It only sees dots: the players and, when the tracker can find it, the ball.

From the raw file to the answer

The whole system is one pass through the match, in seven stages. The rest of this page zooms into stages 4 to 6.

  1. RAW DATA IN

    Dots on a pitch, 25 times a second

    The automatic tracking from the tactical camera gives, for every frame, the position in metres of each person it can see, which team they are on, and the ball when it can find it. No names, no events, nothing typed in by a person. One frame looks like this:

    frame 52,310 Barcelona (x, y) × 11 Athletic (x, y) × 11 ball (x, y) or — not found

    The match notes (Smart Tagging) add a few seconds' window around each set piece and each phase of play. Only the kick-off note is used, to find the start of each half. The rest was tried and left out, for football reasons:

    The phase notes ("Barcelona building up", "Athletic pressing") are padded windows of 15 to 30 seconds. They name the right team about 78% of the time, while the system is already right about 93% of the time: they would teach it worse, not better. The set-piece notes start 3 to 9 seconds before the restart, too loose to time a restart. A rule giving the restart to the tagged taker's team made no measurable difference.

  2. CLEAN

    Throw out what the tracker got wrong

    A ball that jumps metres in one frame, or freezes on one spot, is a tracker glitch and is removed. A missing ball stays missing: nothing is invented (step 7 explains why). The system also notes that this match's ball path has been smoothed, because the touch reading (step 3) adjusts for it.

  3. MEASURE

    Turn dots into the things a coach looks at

    For every frame: who is nearest the ball on each side, how wide and deep each team stands, how fast the quickest players run, whether the ball just changed direction (a touch), how fast and which way it is travelling, and whether it is over a line.

    out: distances to the ballout: team shapesout: running speedsout: touchesout: ball speed and directionout: ball over a line
  4. READ THE CLUES · STEPS 1–8

    Each clue says who is likely on the ball

    Each measurement becomes a vote: Barcelona, Athletic or dead ball. The man on the ball, the duel, the touch, the direction a fast ball is flying, the lines of the pitch, the shapes of the two teams and how fast the players run all have a say. One more vote comes from a learned judge of who holds the visible ball (step 5). The votes from team shapes, player speeds and that judge were learned on other matches, never on this one.

    out: frame by frame: how likely Barcelona, Athletic, dead ball
  5. DECIDE · STEP 10

    One consistent story for each half

    The votes for the whole half are weighed together. Switching team has a cost, so one odd frame never flips possession.

    out: a possession sequence per half
  6. APPLY THE ORGANISERS' RULES · STEP 9

    Tidy the story to match how the game is marked

    Play restarts when the quick players start running. A stoppage starts the moment the ball crossed the line. A touch that falls straight back is not a turnover, and neither is a short spell in which the other team never got the ball under control. Before the kick-off the ball is dead.

    out: final possession sequence

So the question is the one a scout answers from the stand without thinking: who is in control right now? The steps below are how the system rebuilds that judgement from positions alone.

Stages 4 to 6, step by step

  1. STEP 1 · ON THE BALL

    The player within a metre controls it

    If one team has a player within about a metre of the ball and the other does not, that team is in possession. A metre is the reach of a foot: closer than that, the ball is yours to play.

    Football reading: the man on the ball.

  2. STEP 2 · THE DUEL

    In a 50-50, nothing changes until someone comes away with it

    When both teams are within a metre, the system does not flip possession to whoever's boot is nearer. The team that last touched it cleanly keeps it until an opponent has the ball to himself.

    Football reading: a challenge is not a turnover. Winning it is.

  3. STEP 3 · THE TOUCH

    A change in the ball's path means someone played it

    A ball only changes speed or direction sharply when it is struck or controlled. The system finds those moments and credits the nearest player's team. That team then holds possession while the pass travels, until the next touch.

    This match's ball track has been smoothed by the provider. That rounds off every sharp corner, so the ball seems to turn about two metres away from the player who actually played it. The system notices the smoothing and undoes it: it extends the straight line of the ball coming in and the straight line of the ball going out, and where the two lines meet is where the ball was really played. The touch goes to the team standing there.

    Football reading: a pass in flight still belongs to the passer's team.

  4. STEP 4 · WHERE THE BALL IS FLYING

    A fast ball forward usually belongs to the team attacking that way

    Long balls, switches and clearances often travel with nobody near them, and the smoothed track can hide who struck them. But a ball moving fast is rarely going backwards for long: teams mostly play it toward the goal they attack. So while the ball is seen and moving quickly, the team attacking in its direction of travel gets an extra vote. The vote starts small at about 4 metres a second (a firm pass) and reaches full weight at 8 metres a second (a driven ball).

    It is one vote among many, never the answer on its own: on a back-pass to the keeper, the man on the ball and the touch can outvote it.

    Football reading: you can tell who hit a long ball from which way it is going.

  5. STEP 5 · A LEARNED JUDGE

    A second opinion on who holds the visible ball

    On top of the hand-written rules, a simple model gives its own view of which team holds the ball while it is in sight. It looks at what a scout would: which players were near the ball over the last few seconds, not only this instant; which team last kicked it; where it is flying; where on the pitch it is; and the shape of the two teams.

    It was trained only on other matches with known answers. It never saw this match, and nothing in it was adjusted to Barcelona v Athletic. It is given a moderate weight, so it helps settle close calls rather than overruling the man on the ball.

    Football reading: an experienced eye that has watched many matches, asked "whose ball is it?" for each moment.

  6. STEP 6 · DEAD BALL

    The players tell you when play has stopped

    A ball over the line or placed still for a set piece is dead. But the stronger signal is the players: when a game stops, even the quickest players on the pitch drop to a walk and both teams drift into restart positions. The system measures how fast the fastest players are moving and how the two shapes settle.

    When the ball is seen going out, the lines decide the timing, as the Laws do: the stoppage starts at the moment the ball crossed the line, if that happened within the five seconds before the players slowed. And a ball seen more than half a metre outside the lines for two seconds or more is dead ball until it is seen back on the pitch, even when the players have not yet reacted.

    The restart is timed the same way: play is live again at the moment the quick players burst back into a run.

    The start of each half follows the same idea. The second half is marked from a few minutes before the kick-off, while the teams are still lining up. The organisers count all of that as dead ball. The match notes give a five-second window around the kick-off, and the system places the kick-off at the moment inside it when the ball sits on the centre spot. It lands one frame from the real kick-off.

    Football reading: you can tell the whistle has gone without hearing it.

  7. STEP 7 · BALL OUT OF SIGHT

    With no ball, read the two shapes

    For four frames in ten there is no ball to look at. The teams still give it away. The side in possession opens up: it gets wider, its line moves toward the opponent's goal. The side without it closes up: narrow, compact, dropping. The system learned that pattern from other matches where the answer was known, then applies it here.

    It also works out, for this match, how much of the hidden time is stoppage rather than play. When the camera loses the ball it is often because play has stopped.

    Why not draw in the missing ball? It was tried. Placing the hidden ball at the feet of the likely carrier halves the error in where the ball is (about 9 to 11 metres off, against 16 metres for a straight line between the last and next sightings). But it did not make the possession calls any better, because the shapes already answer "whose ball?" more reliably than an invented ball position. So it is not used.

    Football reading: you know who has the ball from the back four alone.

  8. STEP 8 · BALL BACK IN SIGHT

    Whoever is alone on it when it reappears has it

    When the tracker finds the ball again and only one team has a player within a metre, that team is given possession straight away. The system does not assume the team that had it before still has it: across a hidden spell of open play, the ball changes sides almost half the time.

    Football reading: when the camera cuts back, look at who is standing over it.

  9. STEP 9 · A TOUCH IS NOT POSSESSION

    A deflection that comes back is not a turnover

    The organisers' rule: possession only changes when the other team gains control of the ball. A touch without control, a pass clipped by an opponent that is not intercepted, a header won with nobody collecting it: none of these is a change.

    So if Athletic have the ball for less than a second and it comes straight back to Barcelona, that moment stays Barcelona's. If Athletic keep it, the turnover is placed at their first touch, the moment of the interception, not when they settle it.

    There is a second, stricter test. If Athletic have it for under two seconds and in that time no Athletic player ever touched it cleanly (only stabs and pokes in a crowd), that is not control either, and the moment stays Barcelona's.

    Football reading: a block that falls back to the passer's team is not a turnover. An interception that sticks is.

  10. STEP 10 · THE FINAL CALL

    One consistent story for the whole half

    Each clue on its own is jumpy: a deflection, a player running past the ball, a tracking glitch. So the last step weighs every clue across the whole half at once and picks the sequence of possessions that fits them best, with a built-in reluctance to switch. A turnover has to be earned by evidence, not by one odd frame.

    Football reading: an analyst logging possessions, not a fan shouting at every bobble.

How it is marked

The organisers do not count correct frames. They count correct possessions. Each possession the system calls is paired with a real one, and it only counts if the two overlap well in time: same team, started and ended at about the right moment. Calling the right team but switching a few seconds late, or splitting one possession into two, loses marks. A perfect score is 1.0.

The other matches used for practice were labelled with every deflection as a possession of its own, which the organisers' rule does not do. So those answers were re-marked the organisers' way (a team needs the ball for about two seconds to count as in control), and the system is now judged against both versions.

Where it still gets it wrong

Turnovers called late

On a smoothed ball the switch comes a few frames late on average, and about three-quarters of a second late when possession changes in a challenge. The cause was touches the smoothing had hidden; rebuilding the touch point (step 3) recovers many of them. Quick one-twos, with two touches inside half a second, are still read poorly.

Turnovers while the ball is out of sight

When the system names a team during a hidden spell it is right about nine times in ten, but it catches the moment of a hidden turnover within a second only one time in six. A possession under four seconds while the ball is not tracked leaves almost no trace in the team shapes. This is the biggest gap left.

Too many or too few stoppages

In the public five minutes it calls less dead ball than there really is. Over the whole match it calls much more, because it treats long hidden spells as likely stoppages. That cannot be checked without the answers.

Which side restarts

After a stoppage it sometimes gives the restart to the wrong team when both have players near the ball as it comes back into play.

All rules were set on other, public matches. Nothing was fitted to the Barcelona v Athletic answers. The possession, duel and touch rules follow Vidal-Codina et al.; an audit against that paper found its remaining rules do not improve the result. Every term was checked against the organisers' own definitions; steps 6 (kick-off, lines) and 9 (control) bring the system in line with them. The learned judge (step 5) was trained on other matches only. Two steps that adapt to this match's tracking (the smoothed-ball touch reading and the share of hidden time treated as stoppage) are pending confirmation from the organisers.
