How spellF1 sees a possession

A possession is a maximal run of H or of A. Predicted runs are matched one-to-one to true runs by temporal IoU, and F1 is averaged over IoU 0.3–0.7. An X is forward-filled with your last label in that half before matching.

What it tells us

  • Even with perfect data, MatchLens splits possessions: 45–64% too many, each about 40% too short. That is the failure spellF1 punishes.
  • Its dead-ball detection is decent but short of the 0.90 gate.
  • Touch detection and ball kinematics are worth keeping; the frame-by-frame state machine has to be replaced by whole-match segmentation (Viterbi or HSMM).

Limits of this number

  • Metrica truth here comes from event data ("last team to act"), not from corrected tracking as in the challenge, so absolute values will not transfer. Comparisons between versions mostly will.
  • No degradation yet: no lost IDs, no track fragments, ball present 100% of the time. The degraded score will be lower.
  • Saved to eval/results/matchlens_replay_metrica_clean.json; regenerate with scripts/score_matchlens_replay.py.

The provided video was not used: the challenge already supplies tracking, so the possession layer is the part worth testing. The BAR–ATH run read the tactical ATD only, through scripts/blind_run_public5.py, which keeps every per-frame output in data/challenge/_runs/ and prints aggregates only, so our rule that AI tools never see challenge data (docs/RULES.md) held.

Every log and root variant is worse than linear at every gap length, on both matches, and the harder the profile decelerates the worse it gets. A front-loaded profile assumes the gap starts with a kick. The agents' numbers say otherwise: the visible ball moves at a median 4–6 m/s and is almost never still (3% of frames), so a gap starts at a random moment of play, not at a strike. Constant speed is the better average guess; cubic wins because it reads the real speed at both edges.

Reading it

  • On the external match, cubic is the most accurate from 0.5 to 10 s, with degree-3 polynomial close behind. That is the choice the rules allow us to make.
  • On our match, degree 5–7 polynomials win short gaps. Our ball track looks smoothed by the tracker, which rewards high-degree fits. We do not choose on this.
  • Degree 7 falls apart from 5 s on (15–50 m off). Player-anchored is the least accurate as a position, though it is the only method that says which team kept the ball.
  • At 20 s every method is about 19–20 m off: half a pitch. Our real gaps are mostly 10–30 s, so no interpolation can place the ball there.

Limits

  • Hidden stretches come from moments when the ball was seen, which tend to be open play. Real gaps happen in crowds and long balls, so real errors are likely larger.
  • The match has no seen stretches long enough to mimic its own 30 s+ gaps.
  • Position error does not equal possession error; the score depends on the dead-ball and segmentation steps.

The finding that matters: the ball is missing in long blocks

The physics run also counted the gaps in the graded halves (aggregate input statistics only). The 64,091 missing frames sit in just 147 gaps, and the lengths split in two:

Gap lengthGapsMissing framesShare of missingWhat can fill it

This is why linear, cubic, Kalman and physics all score the same: 99.9% of the missing ball sits in gaps of 5 s or more, where drawing a line between two points 20 s apart says nothing about who had the ball. Two consequences. The pipeline must decide possession without the ball for long stretches (steps 4 and 6). And the degradation of external matches must hide the ball in long blocks like these, not at random frames, or our validation will be far too optimistic.

What happened

  • Filling the ball barely moves the score. MatchLens's dead-ball code (deadball.py:331) treats any filled position as "ball missing" and calls the ball dead after 0.5 s, so the filled positions never reach the stoppage logic.
  • Ten fill variants land on exactly two outcomes: 30 possessions (spellF1 0.2383) or 32 (0.2286). The degree of the curve does not matter. Player-anchored adds one useful signal: in 30 of 147 gaps the ball vanished with one team and came back with the other.
  • Three fill methods (linear, cubic, Kalman + RTS) give the same possession structure within noise. The shape of the curve does not matter while the dead-ball rule throws it away.
  • Cubic added two possessions (32 vs 30). One possession moves spellF1 by about 6 points on this window, so 0.238 vs 0.229 is noise, not a ranking.
  • The fill is necessary but not sufficient: "ball missing" has to stop meaning "ball dead" before any method can show its value.

How to read these numbers

  • Public 5 minutes, 17 possessions: a sanity check only. The rules forbid choosing between versions here.
  • The real comparison runs on Metrica and IDSSE matches with the ball hidden like ours, scoring position error per gap length and spellF1 downstream.
  • Per-frame outputs stayed in data/challenge/_runs/; only these aggregate rows were printed.

These are hypotheses to verify on the full match and tune on external data; agent confidence was 20–55%. Full write-up: docs/research/swarm-findings.md.

Where the score comes from

Four levers, in order of impact

1 · Dead balls

Every D stretch ends a possession. Missing one merges two possessions; inventing one splits a possession. Smart Tagging set pieces give restart times almost free.

2 · Segment, don't classify

An HMM or HSMM over {H, A, D} with switch penalties and duration models produces clean runs instead of flicker.

3 · Signals without the ball

Team shape (spread, compactness, pressing line, movement toward the ball) and team-tagged phases need only team membership, not IDs.

4 · Abstain wisely

X inside a possession is free. At a hand-over always commit, or your old label bleeds into the new possession.

Pipeline

Nine steps from tracking to CSV

  1. Read the match. Unify fractional coordinates, per-file frame counts, team membership, tags and homography into one per-frame table.
  2. Clean the ball. Drop one-frame teleports, fill gaps up to about 0.5 s, mark longer gaps unknown. A missing ball does not mean play stopped.
  3. Touches and control. Direction and speed impulses plus the nearest team, then a retention check so a deflection is not counted as a turnover.
  4. Team shape. ID-free team features feed a classifier trained on other matches.
  5. Dead balls. Ball off the pitch, collective slowdown, ball still at a restart spot, kickoff formation, tagged set pieces.
  6. Tags as hints. Smart Tagging phases with a team push the probabilities a little. They are automatic and can be wrong.
  7. Fuse. Per-frame P(H), P(A), P(D), with touches weighted high when the ball is seen and shape plus tags when it is not.
  8. Segment. Whole-match Viterbi or HSMM with switch costs and realistic durations.
  9. Finish. Snap possession starts to the winning touch and D ends to the restart; D before the second-half kickoff; X outside graded time; write 199,319 rows.

  • Hand labels or hand corrections on the provided game, for any purpose.
  • The private ground truth, or any human-produced data about this game (Opta, StatsBomb, corrected tracking).
